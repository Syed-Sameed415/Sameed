The Story

What, Why, and How

Tenebris Coffee — a Kelowna craft coffee company — wanted to bring a café experience to travellers. Their vision: a portable device that heats and froths milk, designed to pair with their canned nitrogen espresso charges. The device needed to eliminate the need for external cups or disposable cartridges, and had to be compact enough to fit in a backpack.

Our team of five took this from a sketch to a working prototype—a 320W heater and a 5V whisk motor, controlled by an Arduino, all in a 3D-printed enclosure.

Key Achievement: The final prototype heated 250mL of milk in under 5 minutes at 77.9% thermal efficiency, produced 75mL of stable foam (target: 30mL), and maintained a safe external shell temperature of 32.5°C.

The Challenge

Problem Scope & Requirements

Existing portable milk frothers either sacrifice consistency for size, or require professional grade steam equipment that's neither portable or affordable.

The initial scope required us to use nitrogen gas from their pressurized espresso cans to froth the milk — a technically demanding constraint that would ultimately reshape our entire design process.

Primary Objectives: Heat 250mL of milk to 60°C in under 5 minutes · Produce café quality microfoam · Fit within a large-thermos form factor (13×30cm) · Remain food safe and portable.

The Solution

Final Design Description

The final prototype is a cylindrical device (12×28cm) consisting of four main subsystems: a stainless steel milk container, a PLA 3D-printed outer housing and lid, a resistive heating element, and a DC frother motor with Arduino controller.

A critical design insight came from earlier prototypes where the heater was placed inside the cup — causing milk to burn on the element. The final design places the heater on the exterior bottom surface of the stainless cup, preventing direct milk contact while maintaining efficient heat transfer.

The control system is simple: no sensors, just preset heating and frothing times based on testing. This reduces failure points and uses only two buttons, one for heat and one for frothing.

Design Insight: Heater positioned on the exterior of the stainless cup — not submerged — prevents milk burning on the element, a failure mode discovered during earlier prototype iterations.

Looking Back

Lessons Learned

This project showed us that client requirements change. Our scope shifted three times, and the real challenge was adapting without losing engineering quality.

We also learned that foam science resists calculation. Unlike heating, which we could model precisely, foam formation depended on milk protein concentration, fat content, temperature, and bubble dynamics in ways that only experimentally testing could accurately model.

Finally, placing the heater outside the cup, discovered by failing with an internal heater, was a reminder that failure is data that you use for future iterations and not neccesarily a setback. Each burnt milk test run informed a better placement decision.

What Worked

  • ✓ External heater placement eliminates milk burning
  • ✓ Feedforward control simplified reliability
  • ✓ Stainless steel cup — thermally efficient + cleanable
  • ✓ O-ring lid seal prevented leakage during operation
