Portfolio / Capstone Project

Liquid Espresso Apparatus 2.0 —
portable milk heater & frother

A client-facing engineering project to design a portable device that heats and froths milk for Tenebris Coffee, a Kelowna-based canned espresso company. Built, tested, and iterated across five client meetings.

CourseENGR 499 — Capstone Design ClientTenebris Coffee, Kelowna BC TeamGroup I33 · 5 members SubmittedApril 2026
What we built and why

Tenebris Coffee wanted a portable device a traveller could use to warm and froth milk and pour it directly into their canned espresso — no cups, no steam wand, no café required. They called it the Liquid Espresso Apparatus, and our job was to take that idea from a brief to a working prototype.

The final device heats 250mL of milk to 60°C using a 320W resistive element and froths it with a 5V DC whisk motor — all controlled by an Arduino and housed in a custom 3D-printed enclosure roughly the size of a large thermos. It completed the task in under 5 minutes with 77.9% thermal efficiency.

Key result Produced 75mL of stable foam (target was 30mL) with 2–3mm bubble diameter — comparable to a café steam wand — while keeping the outer shell at a safe 32.5°C.

How we got there

Our process didn't follow a clean linear path. Every client meeting brought new information — sometimes a changed priority, sometimes a failed test, once a request to abandon months of work entirely. Below is an honest account of how the project evolved.

M1

Scope & concept generation

Defined heating and frothing as two separate subsystems. Identified nitrogen sparging, electric whisk, French press, and planetary gear as candidate frothing methods.

M2

Solution shortlisting

Compared resistive vs. induction heating. Evaluated all four frothing approaches against foam quality, energy use, cost, and portability. Built a weighted decision matrix.

M3

Prototype & first tests

Built Prototype 5 — an electric whisk with AC resistive heater. Ran initial nitrogen sparging tests. Foam from nitrogen was insufficient in quantity and quality at available pressures.

M4

Scope pivot — abandon all prototypes

Client's advisor asked us to abandon the current prototype and shift to research-only. Client then reversed again and insisted nitrogen frothing remain the goal. We re-scoped to a validation + research phase.

M5

Research validation & final decision

Tested sparging wand and sparging stone at 45–90°C and 2–6.3 L/min. Nitrogen definitively could not produce sufficient foam at the pressures available in aluminium cans. Electric whisk re-adopted as final frothing method.

Frothing method comparison
Method Foam quality Portability Cost
Planetary gear whisk High Moderate High Rejected
French press mesh High (with skill) High Low Rejected
Electric DC whisk Good (2–3mm bubbles) Good Low Selected
Nitrogen sparging Excellent (theory only) Low (120 psi walls) Very high Ruled out

Nitrogen frothing requires 120 psi — far above what a standard aluminium can can safely hold. Warm milk also reduces gas solubility, increasing pressure demand further.

Final design

The device is a 12×28cm cylinder. The milk sits in a stainless steel cup (chosen for food safety and thermal conductivity). The resistive heater is mounted on the outside bottom of the cup — a key decision made after early prototypes with internal heaters caused milk to burn on the element. The DC frother motor lives in the PLA lid and drives a whisk submerged in the milk when the lid is placed on.

Control is intentionally simple: two buttons (heater on/off, frother on/off), no sensors, timing based on empirical testing. This eliminates sensor failure modes and keeps the interface accessible to anyone.

Design decision Moving the heater to the exterior of the cup — rather than submerging it — solved the burnt-milk problem discovered in testing. Sometimes the most important design insight comes from a failure, not a calculation.

The two failures are prototype limitations tied to budget and lab constraints, not fundamental design issues. Both are addressed in the next iteration roadmap.

Lessons learned

What worked

  • External heater placement — no burnt milk
  • Feedforward control — simpler and more reliable
  • Stainless cup — efficient, food-safe, easy to clean
  • O-ring lid seal prevented leakage in operation

Next iteration

  • Portable battery with BMS
  • Injection-moulded food-safe plastic
  • Temperature sensor feedback loop
  • Revisit nitrogen at engineered pressures

The biggest lesson was that client requirements are living documents. Our scope changed three times. The teams that struggled were those who held to the original plan when the evidence pointed elsewhere. Staying technically honest while managing client expectations turned out to be as much an engineering skill as any calculation.
