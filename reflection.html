Design Process Attributes of an Engineer Role in Society Strengths & Growth
Question 01

"What did you learn about the design process from your experience in this course?"

This course taught me that the design process is rarely as straightforward as you might expect. Going into the capstone, I expected a clean and structured timeline: define requirements, build, test, deliver. What I came to quickly undertsand was that it more the opposite. Every meeting consisted in one way or another of scope changes, failed hypotheses, and a client who reversed course more than once. As an example, our nitrogen sparging approach, while theoretically sound, collapsed when experimented. We ended up having to take a step back and evaluate our next steps.

What I took away is thatIteration isn’t bad planning, it’s how good engineering happens. Our failures taught us more than calculations: burnt milk showed where to place the heater, and nitrogen tests revealed what’s needed to make the design work at scale.

Question 02

"What are the most important attributes of an engineer?"

From this experience, the most important qualities are adaptability, honesty, and good communication. You need adaptability because plans change, and rigid thinking holds you back. You need honesty because it’s easy to force results—even when something clearly isn’t working.

Communication is underrated. I had to explain technical limits and engineering language to a non technical client and turn their goals into clear tasks for my team.

Question 03

"How has this project contributed to your understanding of the role and responsibilities of the professional engineer in society?"

Working with a real client on a product that will eventually be used by real people showed me a glimpse of what engineering responsibility actually means. Food safety, for example, went from being a checkbox in a design requirements table to something I genuinely had to research and heavily consider as our PLA housing was functional but technically not food safe by ISO 22000 standards. We documented this honestly in our report.

I also came to understand that professional engineers are accountable not just for what they build, but for what they choose not to build and option we choose not to pursue. Ruling out nitrogen frothing at 120 psi, despite the client's preference, was an engineering judgment call that we made as a team. That decision was ours to make and we had to deal with the consequences, including explaining our decision to the client.

Question 04

"What strengths do you bring to your engineering career, and which areas need growth?"

My strongest contributions in this project were in systems integration and cross disciplinary thinking — connecting the electrical, mechanical, and food science constarints of our project. My background in biomedical engineering gave me a good footing with constraints that come from human physiology and safety, which translated well to food safety and thermal considerations.

Where I need to grow is in confident decision-making under stressful and possibly in uncertain conditions. There were moments where I waited for consensus or more data rather than committing to a decision and this ended up costing time. In a professional setting, time is a resource, and hesitation has a cost. I plan to develop this through deliberately taking ownership of technical decisions in future projects and learning to understand that making a decision and ending up finding out it doesnt work rather than wasting time in deliberationa and extending the project timeline.
