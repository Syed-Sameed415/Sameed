What I learned —
and where I still need to grow

A first-person reflection on the design process, engineering as a profession, and what this capstone taught me about myself. Written for ENGR 499, April 2026.

Question 01

What did you learn about the design process from your experience in this course?

I came into capstone expecting a clear path -> define requirements, design, build, test, and deliver. Instead, the project changed direction three times where the client reversed key decisions, experiments didn’t match our calculations, and at one point we were told to pivot from our final goal entirely.

That experience showed me that design isn’t a fixed plan and it’s more of an ongoing iteration of the problem. Our nitrogen sparging idea made sense in theory. The physics supported it but real testing told a different story, and we had to trust the data. That’s harder than it sounds when you’ve already invested time defending an idea.

I also learned that documentation is part of the design process, not just a deliverable at the end. Keeping track of why we made decisions, especially the ones we later reversed, turned out to be essential for explaining the final design to the client and why we chose the route to completion we did.

Question 02

Based on your experiences in this course, what are the most important attributes of an engineer?

Three things stand out to me: adaptability, intellectual honesty, and the ability to communicate.

Adaptability matters because scope changes aren’t rare, they’re just part of the job. The people who struggled most were the ones who treated pivots like failures instead of just a change in direction. And honesty really matters too. It was tempting to make the nitrogen method look like it worked, even when it didn’t. Being honest and saying it failed was one of the most important decisions I made this year.

Communication is probably the most underrated of the three. I had to explain complex constraints to a non technical client, then turn their goals into clear, testable tasks for my team. That translation step is where a lot of projects quietly fall apart.

Question 03

How has your experience on this project contributed to your understanding of the role and responsibilities of the professional engineer in society?

Working with a real client on something people might actually use changed how I see engineering responsibility. Food safety stopped being just a line in a requirements list and became something we had to seriously consider. Our PLA housing worked, but it didn’t meet ISO 22000 standards,and we were honest about that instead of trying to gloss over it.

The more important lesson came from the nitrogen decision. The client wanted nitrogen frothing. We had data showing it wasn't feasible at available pressures. Making that call which was essentially recommending against a clients preference on safety and feasibility grounds, is exactly the kind if experience that showed me the integrity and honesty that comes with being an engineer.

Question 04

What strengths do you bring to your engineering career, and which areas need growth?

My strongest contribution in this project was cotnributing a systems thinking approach —> being able to hold the electrical, mechanical, and food science dimensions of the problem in mind at the same time, and see how a decision in one area affected the others. My biomedical background helped here: I'm used to designs where human safety and biological constraints are paramount, and that mindset translated directly to food safety and thermal comfort considerations.

Where I need to grow is in decisionvmaking under constraints ir ambiguity. There were moments where I waited for more data, or for team consensus, when a clear technical lead was what the situation needed. In a professional context, that hesitation has a cost and that usually end uo being an extension of the overall project timeline. I plan to work on this by actively taking ownership of technical decisions in future projects and by getting comfortable with taking responsibility for these decisions for the betterment of the project.
