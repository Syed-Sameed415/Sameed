What I learned —
and where I still need to grow

A first-person reflection on the design process, engineering as a profession, and what this capstone taught me about myself. Written for ENGR 499, April 2026.

Question 01

What did you learn about the design process from your experience in this course?

I came into the capstone expecting a clean arc: define requirements, design a solution, build it, test it, deliver it. What I got instead was a project that changed direction three times — a client who reversed major decisions mid-stream, experiments that contradicted our calculations, and one meeting where we were asked to abandon months of prototyping entirely.

That experience taught me that the design process is not a plan you execute — it is a conversation you have with the problem. Our nitrogen sparging concept was theoretically sound. The physics backed it. But physical testing at real pressures told a different story, and the honest thing to do was listen to the data. That was harder than it sounds when you have already invested time defending a direction.

"Every failed test is a data point. The burnt-milk experiments told us exactly where to move the heater. Failure became information, not defeat."

I also learned that documentation is part of the design process, not just a deliverable at the end. Keeping track of why we made decisions — especially the ones we later reversed — turned out to be essential for explaining the final design to the client.

Question 02

Based on your experiences in this course, what are the most important attributes of an engineer?

Three things stand out to me: adaptability, intellectual honesty, and the ability to communicate across domains.

Adaptability because scope changes are not exceptions — they are part of the work. The engineers on our team who struggled most were those who saw a pivot as a failure rather than a redirection. Intellectual honesty because the temptation to make the nitrogen method work — to find a way to claim the data supported it — was real. Resisting that temptation, and reporting clearly that it didn't work, was one of the more meaningful professional decisions I made this year.

Communication stands out as the most underrated of the three. I had to translate complex constraints to a client without a technical background, then translate client goals back into testable specifications for my team. That translation work is where a lot of projects fail silently.

Question 03

How has your experience on this project contributed to your understanding of the role and responsibilities of the professional engineer in society?

Working with a real client on a device that real people will eventually use changed how I think about engineering responsibility. Food safety went from a line in a requirements table to something I genuinely had to think through. Our PLA housing was functional, but it didn't meet ISO 22000 standards — and we documented that honestly, rather than glossing over it in the final report.

The more important lesson came from the nitrogen decision. The client wanted nitrogen frothing. We had data showing it wasn't feasible at available pressures. Making that call — recommending against a client preference on safety and feasibility grounds — is exactly the kind of judgment a professional engineer is responsible for. It's not a technical conclusion you can pass off. It's yours to make and to stand behind.

Question 04

What strengths do you bring to your engineering career, and which areas need growth?

My strongest contribution in this project was systems thinking — being able to hold the electrical, mechanical, and food science dimensions of the problem in mind at the same time, and see how a decision in one area affected the others. My biomedical background helped here: I'm used to designs where human safety and biological constraints are non-negotiable, and that mindset translated directly to food safety and thermal comfort considerations.

I'm also a careful, structured communicator. The documentation from this project — the decision logs, the test records, the client meeting summaries — reflects that.

Where I need to grow is in decision-making under ambiguity. There were moments where I waited for more data, or for team consensus, when a clear technical lead was what the situation needed. In a professional context, that hesitation has a cost. I plan to work on this by actively taking ownership of technical decisions in future projects — not just contributing to them — and by getting comfortable with the discomfort of being the person who calls it.
